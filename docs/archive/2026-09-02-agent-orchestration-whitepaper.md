# Supervising many coding agents at once

*A design note on `cdl-linux`, for readers deciding whether to build it.*

## The problem is attention, not capability

One coding agent is easy to supervise. You watch it work, you answer it when it asks, and you read what it did. Nothing about that arrangement scales, and the thing that breaks first is not the machine. Running ten agents at once, the scarce resource is knowing which one is waiting for you.

The failure is quiet, which is what makes it expensive. An agent that finishes in four minutes and then sits unnoticed for forty has cost you thirty-six minutes of wall time that no dashboard reports. Moreover, an agent that hit an approval prompt looks (exactly) like an agent that is thinking hard. In other words, the state you most need to see, i.e. *blocked on a human*, is the state hardest to distinguish from ordinary progress, and no terminal multiplexer we know of surfaces it. For example, a pane running an agent that has been waiting on a yes-or-no question for twenty minutes renders identically to one mid-refactor.

The usual answers do not survive contact with the problem. Terminal multiplexers keep processes alive and answer none of the supervisory questions: `zellij` and `tmux` will not tell you which pane is waiting, they hold scrollback in memory (so the record of what an agent did dies with the session), and they cannot see a machine you are not sitting at. CI systems answer the reporting question but assume the work is non-interactive, which agents are not. Hosted agent platforms answer both, at the cost of running your code and holding your credentials on someone else's machine. Each does something well. None of them treats *which of my sixteen units needs me right now* as the question the system exists to answer.

`cdl-linux` is a Linux distribution built around that question. The bet is that a coordination appliance for concurrent agent work is a different product from a general-purpose desktop with agents installed on it, and that the difference shows up in the parts nobody enjoys building: crash recovery, ownership of shared state, and knowing what happened after the fact.

## What we are building, and what we are not

The system supervises **units of work**. A unit is either an *agent* (interactive, holds a terminal, expected to block on human input) or a *job* (batch, no terminal, may produce artifacts). Both share one state machine, one registry, and one recovery path, which is what lets a single `cdl status` table show local agents and remote jobs together. In addition, one shared path means `cdl reconcile` is one piece of code with per-backend probes, rather than a separate implementation per location.

The target hardware is one laptop, measured rather than assumed: a Lambda TensorBook with 64 GB of RAM, an RTX 3080 Laptop GPU with 16 GB of VRAM, and two 1 TB NVMe drives. We designed against roughly sixteen concurrent units across three places (5 to 10 API-backed agents on the laptop, 1 to 2 local-model agents, and 2 to 4 jobs on a lab GPU host reached over SSH). That number drove more decisions than any other, because three execution locations force (a) a port registry, (b) per-agent resource limits, and (c) a reconciliation path that a single-machine design would not need. Finally, it is also what makes attention the binding constraint rather than compute: sixteen units on one screen is more than anyone tracks unaided.

Four agent CLIs are in scope (Claude Code, Codex, Gemini and OpenCode), behind an adapter interface that isolates what differs between them: how to launch one, how to tell it is waiting, how to read its spend, and which sandbox flags make it fail closed. Local inference is served by `llama-swap` in front of `llama.cpp`, presenting one endpoint that loads and unloads models on demand. Conceptually, the adapter layer exists so that adding a fifth CLI (e.g. `goose`, which is Apache-2.0 and already packaged) is a new file rather than a redesign.

Several things are deliberately out of scope. We are not building an agent, a model, or a prompt framework. We are not building a hosted service or a multi-user system (the threat model assumes one person at one machine). We are not trying to make agents safe to run unattended against arbitrary code. Instead, we are trying to make it obvious what they did, and cheap to recover when one goes wrong.

## How it works

**Lifecycle position and outcome are separate.** A unit is in one of six states (`queued`, `starting`, `running`, `waiting`, `stopping`, `terminal`) and a terminal unit carries one of six outcomes (`completed`, `failed`, `launch_failed`, `cancelled`, `budget_exceeded`, `lost`). Encoding every failure mode as its own state is what makes a state machine grow without bound; separating the two means new failure modes add an outcome and leave the transition graph alone. `waiting` is a first-class state rather than a flag, because "which unit needs me?" should be a `WHERE` clause rather than a scan over parsed status text. Also, detection is best-effort and the design says so: a missed `waiting` costs attention (not correctness), and `cdl status` marks the units whose waiting state came from a timing heuristic rather than from a pattern the adapter reliably recognizes. `lost` is deliberately distinct from `failed`: "we do not know what happened" and "it returned 3" are different facts, and collapsing them turns post-crash triage into guesswork.

**Exactly one actor owns a row at a time.** A local SQLite database in WAL mode is the authority, and five kinds of process write it (a CLI, an admission daemon, one supervisor per unit, one controller per remote host, and a reconciler). Ownership transfers at defined points rather than being shared: the CLI creates a `queued` row and exits, admission owns it until the supervisor acknowledges its own launch, and the supervisor owns it from there. Cancellation is a *request* (a timestamp on the row) rather than a transition, so whichever actor currently owns the unit is the one that acts on it. For example, cancelling a unit that is still queued is handled by admission and never starts a process at all, which is why no signal is sent and no grace period is served. For example again, a spawn failure is recorded by admission (which still owns the row) rather than being discovered later by a probe that can only say the process is missing.

**The supervisor owns the terminal, not the multiplexer.** Each unit runs under a `cdl`-owned supervisor process that holds the PTY master for the unit's whole life and appends every byte to a durable log, whether or not anyone is attached. Attaching is a thin client over a unix socket. Killing your terminal, or every terminal, cannot `SIGHUP` the agent, and the record of what it did outlives the session. Multiple clients may attach, but exactly one is the writer and the others are read-only observers with an explicit takeover command, because two clients writing to one PTY interleave keystrokes, and two window sizes fight over `TIOCSWINSZ` with no error anywhere to notice. In other words, the arbitration exists to stop one person fighting themselves across two terminals, which with sixteen units is the ordinary case (rather than the exotic one).

**Recovery assumes the row is lying.** Any row claiming to be alive may be false after a reboot, an OOM kill or a restore from backup, and nothing in the row itself reveals it. Reconciliation therefore probes, and it dispatches on *where the process runs* before it checks anything: a laptop's boot ID says nothing about a job on the GPU host, and an earlier draft that checked it first would have marked every live remote job `lost` on every reboot. For remote units the terminal result is imported before any liveness check, because the commonest remote event is a job finishing, and a finished job has no process to find. In other words, an earlier draft could not (reliably) tell a job that had succeeded from one that had died, and it resolved both to "lost".

**Isolation is by construction where we can manage it.** Each agent gets its own git worktree and runs under `bubblewrap` with other worktrees, the keystore and the registry *not mounted at all* rather than mounted and denied, so a permission bug has nothing to read through. Provider credentials are built per process and never written to a shell profile or a global environment file. This follows from measured behaviour rather than taste: Claude Code's phone-steering feature refuses to run when `ANTHROPIC_BASE_URL` points anywhere but the vendor's endpoint, so the obvious distro design (i.e. one gateway, set globally) would silently remove a capability the user paid for. Network policy ships in two stages: the first slice runs with no network namespace connectivity at all, and controlled egress through a hostname-allow-listing proxy comes later.

## What to push on before we build this

We would rather hear about these now than after the code exists. They are ordered by how much damage a wrong answer does.

**The premise may not survive daily use.** The design assumes two things: that one person sustainably supervises about sixteen concurrent units, and that attention rather than review capacity is the binding constraint. Suppose the ceiling is four agents, because nobody can meaningfully review more than four diffs an hour. Most of this machinery would then be solving a problem that does not arise, and a far smaller tool would do. What would settle it: run the first slice against a test adapter and count how many units one person can keep supplied with decisions, before the remote and GPU layers get built.

**One worktree per agent rests on a number we have not reproduced.** The justification is a reported experiment in which agents sharing one checkout landed 24 of 100 commits with 75 `index.lock` failures, against 100 of 100 with a worktree each. That figure came from a research subagent and we have not independently reproduced it. It matters because worktree-per-agent drives the port registry, the seeding step, and a good deal of the cleanup logic. What would settle it: rerun the collision experiment directly, which is a few hours of work and should happen before the registry is built rather than after.

**Building a distribution is a much larger commitment than building a tool.** Nothing in the architecture above requires a custom ISO. It requires a supervisor, a database and a CLI, all of which would run on stock Ubuntu. The distribution work (installer, package repository, signing, updates) is where projects like this one usually die, and we cannot yet say whether the appliance framing earns its cost, or whether this should ship as a package (i.e., something that installs onto a machine someone already has). The distribution is currently planned last, after the environment has proved itself in daily work. That ordering is a hedge rather than an answer.

**The hardware fights us on one launch requirement.** Hibernation is a stated requirement and is currently unavailable: Secure Boot puts the kernel in `integrity` lockdown mode, which hides `disk` from `/sys/power/state`. We confirmed the cause by direct read rather than inference, and confirmed that Secure Boot can be toggled reversibly on this firmware, so the trade (a verified boot chain versus working hibernation) can be measured rather than argued. Separately, the machine has logged 65 and 66 unsafe shutdowns across 255 and 206 power-on hours, and we have found a plausible mechanism (USB wake armed at S3) but have not yet caught it happening. What would settle both: one suspend-resume cycle with logging, and one boot with Secure Boot disabled.

**Spend control is weaker than the word "budget" suggests.** `cdl` decides whether a unit starts; it does not sit between an agent and its provider, so it cannot refuse an individual API call. A per-unit budget is a stop-loss rather than a cap: overshoot is bounded by one request's cost plus the detection interval, not by zero. Several CLIs report cost only after a response and some not at all, so part of the accounting is estimated from a shipped price table, and an estimated figure never triggers a hard stop. This looks to us like the ceiling for any system that does not proxy provider traffic. A reviewer who disagrees should say so, since the alternative (an interception proxy) is a far larger piece of machinery.

**Finally, one threat is defined but not closed in the first slice.** An agent permitted to reach its provider can send that provider anything in its worktree. Hostname allow-listing closes exfiltration to an *arbitrary* host and cannot close it to a *permitted* one, and a `CONNECT` proxy cannot see inside a tunnel. Closing that would require terminating TLS with an interception certificate on the machine, which is a larger and more dangerous capability than the one it defends against. We have chosen not to, explicitly, rather than by omission.

## Where this stands

The component described here is specified in detail (a 2,200-line design document) and none of it is implemented. That ordering is deliberate: the expensive things to get wrong are the state machine, the ownership rules and the recovery semantics, and those are cheaper to argue about on paper than to refactor out of a running system.

The specification has been through six review rounds, three of them by reviewers with no prior context who were given the document and nothing else. That last detail changed the results more than any other methodological choice: the cold rounds found more defects than the informed ones, not fewer, including two that would have destroyed live work (reconciliation marking healthy remote jobs as lost, and a launch race that could leave a running agent behind a terminated database row). Twenty-three executable tests now extract the schema from the specification and run it, because across those rounds the longest-surviving defects were all cases where the prose and the schema disagreed and only executing the SQL could tell.

What we would like from a reader: a judgement on the premise, an opinion on whether the distribution framing justifies what it will cost, and any failure mode in the ownership or recovery rules that six rounds have not surfaced.
